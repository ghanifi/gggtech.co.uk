Hotel IT Support Costs in the UK:
Pricing Models Explained

Per device, per room, fixed monthly or pay-as-you-go? How hotel IT support is priced, what pushes the cost up, the hidden charges to watch for and how to budget with confidence.

GG

GGG Technologies Team

Hotel IT Specialists · London, UK

Share

"How much will IT support cost?" is usually the first question a hotel asks, and the honest answer is "it depends". That is frustrating, but it is also true: two hotels with the same number of rooms can need very different levels of support depending on their systems, their opening hours, where they are and how old their equipment is.

What you can do is understand how hotel IT support is priced and what pushes the price up or down. Once you know that, you can read quotes properly, compare them fairly and avoid the surprises that turn a reasonable monthly fee into an expensive year. This guide explains the common pricing models used in the UK, the factors that drive cost, the hidden charges to look out for, and how to build a sensible IT budget for your property.

The Six Common Pricing Models

Most providers use one of the following models, or a blend of them. None is inherently better; each suits different properties and carries different risks.

1. Per-device pricing

How it works: You pay a monthly fee for each supported device: PCs, laptops, servers, tills, switches, access points and so on. Different device types often carry different rates.

Suits: Properties with a stable, well-documented estate where the device count rarely changes.

Watch for: What counts as a "device". Are card terminals, door-lock encoders, CCTV recorders and smart TVs included? Hotels have many devices that are not computers, and a quote that only counts PCs can look cheap until the rest are added.

2. Per-user pricing

How it works: A monthly fee for each member of staff who uses IT, regardless of how many devices they use.

Suits: Office-style teams, head offices and sales or reservations teams where each person has their own account.

Watch for: Hotels rely heavily on shared workstations at reception, in the restaurant and in back-of-house areas, and on seasonal and agency staff. Per-user pricing can be hard to apply fairly to a front desk used by a rotating team across three shifts, and it says nothing about the network and infrastructure that guests depend on.

3. Per-room pricing

How it works: A monthly fee based on the number of guest rooms, sometimes with a separate element for public areas and conference space.

Suits: Hotels that want a figure that scales with the size of the business and is easy to explain to owners. It is common for guest WiFi services, where room count directly affects access points and bandwidth.

Watch for: Room count is only a rough proxy for IT complexity. A 40-room boutique hotel with a restaurant, spa and events space may need more support than a 120-room budget property with limited services.

4. Per-site fixed monthly fee

How it works: A single agreed monthly fee covering a defined scope of support for the whole property, based on a survey of its systems and needs.

Suits: Most hotels that want predictable costs and an all-inclusive relationship. It is the model that best aligns the provider's incentives with yours: they benefit from preventing problems, not from billing for them.

Watch for: The exact scope. "Fully managed" should be defined in writing: which systems, which hours, whether on-site visits are included, and what counts as a project rather than support.

5. Break-fix or ad-hoc hourly

How it works: No contract. You call when something breaks and pay for the time and any parts used, usually with a minimum charge and higher rates out of hours.

Suits: Very small properties with simple setups, or as a temporary arrangement while you choose a provider.

Watch for: Break-fix looks cheap in a quiet month and expensive in a bad one. More importantly, nobody is paid to monitor, patch or prevent problems, so issues tend to be found by guests and staff rather than by your IT provider. There is also no guaranteed response: at 2am on a Saturday you are relying on availability, not commitment.

6. Project fees

How it works: A fixed or estimated price for a defined piece of work, such as a WiFi upgrade, a PMS migration, a new comms cabinet or a refurbishment fit-out.

Suits: One-off changes that sit outside day-to-day support. Most hotels pay project fees alongside a monthly support agreement.

Watch for: Whether the quote includes hardware, cabling, licences, out-of-hours working (often essential in a live hotel), project management and post-installation support. Ask what happens if the scope changes once work starts.

What Drives the Cost of Hotel IT Support

Property size and layout

More rooms, floors and buildings mean more access points, switches, cabling and devices to manage. Layout matters too: a hotel spread across several older buildings or with thick walls needs more infrastructure than a modern block of the same size.

Number and complexity of systems

Every additional system adds support effort, and every integration between systems adds more. A property running a PMS, channel manager, EPOS, spa booking, door locks, IPTV, telephony and CCTV with interfaces between them is a very different proposition from one running a cloud PMS and little else. Providers with PMS integration experience will price this more accurately because they understand where the work actually is.

24/7 cover and on-site SLAs

This is often the single biggest variable. A remote-only service during business hours costs far less to deliver than a 24/7 helpdesk backed by guaranteed out-of-hours on-site attendance. Hotels need round-the-clock cover for critical systems, but you can sometimes save by agreeing different response levels for critical and non-critical issues, and by making the most of remote support where it is genuinely sufficient.

Location: London versus regional

Engineer time, travel, parking and congestion all affect the cost of on-site support. Central London properties often face higher labour and access costs, while remote rural properties may face longer travel times that affect how quickly an engineer can realistically arrive. Ask providers where their engineers are based relative to your hotel; our London hotel IT support page explains how we cover the capital.

Security and compliance scope

Basic antivirus and patching is one thing. Managed firewalls, network segmentation, multi-factor authentication, security monitoring, staff awareness training, and evidence to support PCI DSS compliance or Cyber Essentials certification are another. These are worth paying for (our article on security threats facing UK hotels explains why), but they should be priced and scoped explicitly so you know what you are getting.

Legacy and unsupported equipment

Old servers, out-of-support operating systems, consumer-grade WiFi and undocumented cabling take more time to support and fail more often. Some providers price this risk into the monthly fee; others exclude legacy kit or recommend replacing it first. Either way, ageing equipment costs you money, whether through higher support fees or more downtime.

Hidden Costs to Watch For

  • Out-of-hours surcharges. Some contracts include remote out-of-hours support but bill every night-time or weekend site visit separately.
  • Minimum call-out charges. A five-minute fix billed as a two- or four-hour minimum.
  • Onboarding fees. One-off charges to take over from your previous provider, document the estate or install monitoring tools.
  • "Project" reclassification. Routine work such as setting up a new starter, moving a PC or adding an access point charged as a project.
  • Third-party vendor time. Charges for time spent on the phone to your PMS, EPOS or lock supplier.
  • Licences and hardware margins. Software subscriptions and equipment bundled into the price without clear itemisation.
  • Exit charges. Fees to hand over passwords, documentation and configurations when you leave.
  • Auto-renewal. Contracts that roll over for another full term unless you give notice within a narrow window.
  • The cost of downtime. Not on the invoice, but real: lost bookings, comped rooms, poor reviews and staff time when systems fail. A cheaper contract with slow response can cost more overall.

In-House vs Outsourced IT: A Comparison

Some hotels, particularly larger properties and groups, employ their own IT staff. Others outsource entirely. Many combine the two. Here is how the approaches compare.

A hybrid model, with an in-house IT lead supported by an external provider for out-of-hours cover, security and projects, is often the most resilient option for larger hotels.

How to Budget for Hotel IT Support

  1. Separate running costs from projects. Budget the monthly support fee as an operating cost, and plan hardware replacement and upgrades as a separate capital line.
  2. Build a hardware lifecycle plan. List PCs, servers, switches, firewalls and access points with their age, and plan replacements before they become unsupported. Spreading refreshes over several years avoids a single large bill.
  3. Include connectivity and licences. Internet circuits, software subscriptions and support contracts with system vendors (PMS, EPOS, locks) belong in the IT budget even if they are invoiced by other suppliers.
  4. Set aside a contingency. Unexpected failures, new regulations and opportunities will arise. A modest contingency stops them derailing the year.
  5. Price the cost of failure. Estimate what an hour of PMS or WiFi downtime costs your property at full occupancy. It turns IT budgeting from a cost discussion into a risk discussion.
  6. Review annually. Compare actual spend with budget, look at recurring issues in your provider's reports, and adjust scope accordingly.

Comparing Quotes Like-for-Like

Because providers use different models, headline prices are rarely comparable. To compare properly:

  • Give every provider the same written brief: rooms, users, devices, systems and required hours of cover.
  • Ask each to state response times by priority, and what is and is not included out of hours.
  • Ask for the estimated total annual cost, including likely call-outs, onboarding and licences, not just the monthly fee.
  • Check contract length, notice period and exit terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does hotel IT support cost in the UK?

There is no single figure. Cost depends on the size of the property, the number of systems, the level of 24/7 and on-site cover, location, security requirements and the age of your equipment. The most reliable way to find out is a site assessment followed by an itemised quote.

Which pricing model is best for a hotel?

Most hotels benefit from a fixed monthly fee per site, because it gives predictable costs and encourages the provider to prevent problems. Per-room or per-device pricing can also work if inclusions are clearly defined. Break-fix alone rarely suits a 24/7 property.

Is break-fix IT support cheaper?

It can be cheaper in a quiet month, but there is no monitoring, no prevention and no guaranteed response. For a hotel, a single serious outage can outweigh the savings, so break-fix is usually only suitable for very small, simple properties.

What should a hotel IT support contract include?

At minimum: defined response times by priority, 24/7 cover for critical systems, on-site attendance terms, monitoring and patching, backup management, vendor liaison, security measures, regular reporting and clear exit terms. Anything excluded should be listed in writing.
