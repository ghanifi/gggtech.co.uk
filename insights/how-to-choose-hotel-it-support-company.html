How to Choose a Hotel IT Support Company
in the UK (2026 Checklist)

Hotels are not offices. Here are the questions to ask, the red flags to avoid and a scoring checklist to help you pick an IT partner that can keep a 24/7 property running.

GGG Technologies Team

Hotel IT Specialists · London, UK

Choosing an IT support company is one of the few decisions a hotel makes that touches every department at once. Front office depends on the PMS. Restaurants and bars depend on EPOS and card terminals. Housekeeping, maintenance and security depend on the network. Guests judge you on the WiFi. When IT fails at 2am on a sold-out Saturday, the provider you picked is the difference between a five-minute fix and a lobby full of guests who cannot get into their rooms.

This guide is written for general managers, owners, finance directors and operations leads who are either appointing their first IT partner or replacing one that is not working. It explains why hotels are harder to support than ordinary offices, the questions that separate genuine hospitality specialists from generalists, the red flags to walk away from, and a scoring checklist you can use to compare proposals side by side.

Why Generic IT Providers Struggle with Hotels

Many managed service providers (MSPs) are excellent at supporting offices: fifty laptops, Microsoft 365, a file server and a printer that works from nine to five. A hotel looks similar on paper but behaves very differently in practice. These are the areas where office-focused providers most often come unstuck.

24/7 operations with no quiet period

A hotel never closes. Late check-ins, night audit, early breakfast service and overnight security all depend on systems working at hours when an office-focused helpdesk has gone home. A provider whose "24/7 support" means an answerphone and a ticket picked up at 8:30am is not offering hotel support. You need people who answer the phone, can connect remotely, and can get an engineer on site when remote help is not enough.

The PMS sits at the centre of everything

Your property management system is connected to far more than reservations. It typically talks to the channel manager, booking engine, EPOS, call accounting, key-card encoders, the guest WiFi portal, in-room TV and often revenue management tools. When something breaks, the fault is frequently in an interface between two systems rather than in either system itself. A provider without PMS integration experience will spend hours bouncing tickets between vendors while your front desk works from paper.

Payment terminals and PCI DSS

Hotels take card payments at reception, in restaurants and bars, over the phone and through online bookings, and often hold card details for pre-authorisations and no-show charges. That puts you firmly in scope for PCI DSS, the card industry's security standard. PCI DSS v4 (currently v4.0.1) is now the only version in force, and its previously future-dated requirements became mandatory on 31 March 2025. Your IT provider does not make you compliant on their own, but how they configure networks, firewalls, remote access and user accounts has a direct effect on your compliance scope and your risk. Ask how they approach PCI DSS compliance for hotels specifically.

Guest WiFi is a public service, not an office network

Office WiFi supports known staff devices. Hotel WiFi supports hundreds of unknown devices that arrive and leave every day, expect to stream video in the evening peak, and must be kept strictly separate from your operational systems. Coverage through thick walls, captive portals, bandwidth management and conference-day spikes are specialist problems. Our guide to hotel WiFi problems covers what goes wrong when guest networks are designed like office networks.

Door locks, CCTV and building systems

Electronic door locks and key-card encoders, CCTV, access control, digital signage, telephony and even lift and HVAC controllers increasingly live on the same network. Each comes from a different manufacturer with its own support line. A generic MSP will often say "that's not IT" and leave you to chase the lock vendor yourself. A hotel specialist expects to work with these systems and knows which network, power and integration problems affect them.

Many vendors, one point of accountability

A typical hotel might deal with a PMS vendor, an EPOS supplier, a payment provider, an internet service provider, a lock manufacturer, a TV system supplier and a telephony company. When something fails, the most valuable thing an IT partner can do is take ownership: diagnose where the fault really is, raise it with the right vendor, and chase it until it is fixed. Ask every shortlisted provider how they handle third-party vendor management, and whether it is included or charged extra.

The Questions to Ask Before You Sign

Proposals tend to look alike. The way to separate providers is to ask specific questions and listen carefully to how specific the answers are.

1. What exactly is your response SLA?

  • Is the commitment a response time (someone acknowledges and starts work) or a resolution or on-site arrival time? These are very different promises.
  • How are incidents prioritised? A PMS outage at check-in should be treated differently from a printer jam in the back office. Ask to see the priority definitions.
  • Does the same SLA apply at night, at weekends and on bank holidays?
  • How is performance measured and reported, and what happens if the SLA is missed?

2. Who comes on site out of hours, and how quickly?

Remote support resolves most issues, but not all of them. A failed switch, a dead access point or a damaged cable needs hands on site. Ask where the nearest engineers are based, whether out-of-hours call-outs are included or billed separately, and whether engineers carry common spares. For properties in the capital, check genuine coverage across London rather than a postcode list. Our on-site support and remote support pages explain how the two work together.

3. Which hotel systems have you supported?

Ask which PMS, EPOS, door-lock and WiFi platforms they have worked with, and what they actually do with them. Supporting the Windows PC the PMS runs on is not the same as understanding its interfaces, night audit process or vendor escalation route. Ask them to talk you through how they would troubleshoot a broken PMS-to-EPOS posting interface. Their answer will tell you a lot.

4. How will you take over from our current provider?

Transition is where many relationships start badly. A good provider will describe a structured onboarding: discovery and site survey, documentation of every device, licence and password, agreed handover with the outgoing provider, monitoring agents deployed, and a clear go-live date. Ask what they need from you, how long it typically takes for a property like yours, and how they handle an outgoing provider who is slow to cooperate.

5. How do you handle security and compliance?

  • Do they separate guest, staff, payment and building-systems networks with proper segmentation?
  • How is their own remote access to your systems secured? Look for multi-factor authentication, named accounts and logging.
  • How are patches, backups and antivirus managed and evidenced?
  • Will they support your PCI DSS self-assessment and, if relevant, Cyber Essentials certification?
  • What happens if there is a breach? Do they have an incident response process, and will they help you meet UK GDPR reporting obligations?

For context on the threats you are defending against, see our article on critical security threats facing UK hotels.

6. What reporting and reviews do we get?

You should be able to see what you are paying for. Ask for an example monthly report: ticket volumes by category, SLA performance, recurring issues, devices nearing end of life, security status and recommendations. Ask whether there is a named account manager and how often service reviews take place.

7. What are the contract terms?

  • Contract length, notice period and whether it auto-renews.
  • Exactly what is included and what is chargeable: out-of-hours visits, projects, new starters, vendor liaison, hardware.
  • Exit terms: will they hand over all documentation, passwords and configurations promptly and at no extra cost if you leave?
  • Who owns the hardware, licences and domain names they set up?

Red Flags to Walk Away From

  • Vague SLAs. "Fast response" or "best endeavours" without numbers, priorities or out-of-hours detail.
  • No hotel references. Plenty of office clients but nobody running a 24/7 property you can speak to.
  • "That's the vendor's problem." Reluctance to own issues involving the PMS, locks, EPOS or ISP.
  • A flat network. If they do not raise segmentation between guest, staff and payment networks during the survey, they are unlikely to fix it later.
  • Shared admin passwords. Engineers using one generic login across clients is a security and accountability failure.
  • No documentation on exit. Contracts that are silent on handover, or that charge to release your own passwords and configurations.
  • Pricing that cannot be explained. If you cannot work out what a call-out at 3am on a Sunday will cost, assume it will be expensive.
  • No site visit before quoting. A credible provider wants to see your comms room, cabling and systems before committing to an SLA.

How to Run the Selection Process

  1. List what you have. Number of rooms, staff users, PCs, tills, card terminals, access points, switches, servers and the key systems (PMS, EPOS, locks, TV, telephony). Note the pain points you are trying to solve.
  2. Shortlist three providers. Include at least one hospitality specialist. More than three makes like-for-like comparison hard.
  3. Insist on a site visit. Walk them round the comms cupboard, reception, back office and a typical guest floor.
  4. Score proposals using the checklist above, with at least two people from different departments scoring independently.
  5. Take up references from other hotels, and ask specifically about out-of-hours incidents and how the provider behaved when something went wrong.
  6. Read the contract with particular attention to SLAs, exclusions, notice periods and exit terms before you sign.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a general IT company support a hotel?

It can, but many struggle with the 24/7 nature of hotel operations and with hospitality-specific systems such as the PMS, door locks, EPOS and guest WiFi. If you are considering a generalist, test them hard on out-of-hours on-site cover, hotel system experience and vendor management.

What response time should a hotel expect from IT support?

Response times should be defined by priority. Critical issues such as a PMS, network or payment outage need an immediate response at any hour, with on-site attendance if remote help cannot fix it. Lower-priority issues can reasonably wait longer. Check that the same commitments apply at night and at weekends.

How long does it take to switch hotel IT providers?

It depends on the size of the property, the number of systems and how well the current setup is documented. A good provider will give you a written transition plan with timings after their site survey, and will manage the handover from your outgoing provider.

Does my IT support company make us PCI DSS compliant?

No single supplier makes a hotel compliant; compliance is the hotel's responsibility as the merchant. However, your IT provider controls much of the network and access configuration that determines your PCI DSS scope and risk, so they should actively support your compliance work.

Should a hotel have in-house IT staff or outsource?

Larger properties sometimes keep an in-house IT manager and outsource out-of-hours cover, specialist projects and security. Smaller hotels usually find a fully managed service more practical. Our hotel IT support costs guide compares the two approaches.
